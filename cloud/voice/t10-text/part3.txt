But the fee wasn't the only surprise waiting at the end of the meal. The screen was.

You know the one. Somebody spins the tablet around, and it's already showing you three buttons. Twenty. Twenty-two. Twenty-five. At a counter where you ordered your own food and carried it to your own table.

Bankrate asked Americans about this last year. Forty-one percent said tipping culture is out of control. That's up six points in a single year. And nearly two in five said the pre-set tip screen is exactly the thing that bugs them.

So people did what people do. They pushed back. One survey this year found that thirty-five percent of diners have cut how often or how much they tip. The screen that was supposed to squeeze out a few more dollars? Now it's squeezing the server.

And the fees make it worse. In that same Technomic survey, more than a quarter of diners said that if they saw a service fee, they'd assume it was the tip, and wouldn't tip on top. Except in a lot of restaurants, it isn't the tip. So the owner adds a fee, the customer skips the tip, and the person who carried your plates goes home with less. Everybody loses. Except the receipt.

And when the industry tried to get even cleverer, customers caught that too. In 2024, the new CEO of Wendy's told investors the chain would test something called dynamic pricing on its new digital menu boards. The internet heard two words. Surge pricing. A burger that costs more at lunch, like an Uber in the rain.

About two weeks later, Wendy's put out a statement. It would not raise prices when demand is highest. Burger King jumped right in with a promotion called No Urge to Surge.

The lesson? Customers are a lot harder to fool than the pricing people hoped. They just don't make a scene. They leave. [PAUSE]
