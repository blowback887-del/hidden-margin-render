[VISUAL: a fork in the road graphic — "RAISE THE MENU" vs "ADD A LINE"]

A lot of them picked door number two. A service fee. A kitchen appreciation fee. An operations charge. An admin fee. One bar in Minnesota added five percent for employee health care. And then, on one visit, a dollar for extra crackers with the artichoke dip. [PAUSE] On paper, it's genius. The menu still says twenty-four seventy-five. Nobody gets scared off at the door.
There's just one problem. The customer still reads the receipt.

## PART 3
[VISUAL: a tablet spinning around toward the camera]
But the fee wasn't the only surprise waiting at the end of the meal. The screen was.
You know the one. Somebody spins the tablet around, and it's already showing you three buttons. Twenty. Twenty-two. Twenty-five. At a counter where you ordered your own food and carried it to your own table.
[VISUAL: Bankrate chart 35% → 41%]
Bankrate asked Americans about this last year. Forty-one percent said tipping culture is out of control. That's up six points in a single year. And nearly two in five said the pre-set tip screen is exactly the thing that bugs them.
[VISUAL: Popmenu stat; a server looking at a small tip]
So people did what people do. They pushed back. One survey this year found that thirty-five percent of diners have cut how often or how much they tip. The screen that was supposed to squeeze out a few more dollars? Now it's squeezing the server.
[VISUAL: Technomic stat — "more than 1 in 4 think the fee IS the tip"]
And the fees make it worse. In that same Technomic survey, more than a quarter of diners said that if they saw a service fee, they'd assume it was the tip, and wouldn't tip on top. Except in a lot of restaurants, it isn't the tip. So the owner adds a fee, the customer skips the tip, and the person who carried your plates goes home with less. Everybody loses. Except the receipt.
[VISUAL: 2024 headlines about Wendy's "surge pricing", digital menu boards]
And when the industry tried to get even cleverer, customers caught that too. In 2024, the new CEO of Wendy's told investors the chain would test something called dynamic pricing on its new digital menu boards. The internet heard two words. Surge pricing. A burger that costs more at lunch, like an Uber in the rain.
[VISUAL: Wendy's statement on screen; Burger King "No urge to surge" promo]
About two weeks later, Wendy's put out a statement. It would not raise prices when demand is highest. Burger King jumped right in with a promotion called No Urge to Surge.
The lesson? Customers are a lot harder to fool than the pricing people hoped. They just don't make a scene. They leave. [PAUSE]

## PART 5
[VISUAL: empty dining rooms; then one packed dining room]
So here's the question. If everybody is losing customers, how did one big chain win them?
[VISUAL: the X post with the Burger King menu photo, $18.19 circled]
This summer, a different guy posted a photo of a different price. A Bacon King meal on a Burger King menu. Eighteen dollars and nineteen cents. His caption: why should I get this when I could just go to a restaurant?
[VISUAL: Chili's reply on X, view counter climbing]
And a restaurant answered him. Chili's. This is what we've been saying. Then: why do y'all let fast food play you like this? A chicken sandwich, fries, bottomless chips and salsa, and an unlimited drink. Ten ninety-nine. That reply got more than five million views.
[VISUAL: Chili's 3 for Me ad, the pepper mascot banging on the window]
Here's the twist. While other places were hiding money in the fine print, Chili's put one price in giant letters and dared you to compare. Three for Me. Ten ninety-nine. It wasn't an accident. Over the decade before, the big fast food chains had raised their prices by about sixty percent on average, according to one analysis. So in 2024, Chili's picked a fight. A new burger that Chili's says has twice the beef of a Big Mac, at a price a drive-thru couldn't match. They even ran an ad where a pepper mascot bangs on the window, begging for a job. Because, the joke goes, Chili's doesn't pay for mascots.
[VISUAL: Brinker earnings: +24% sales, +16% guests; then +21%, +13%]
And the customers came. From April to June of 2025, sales at existing Chili's jumped twenty-four percent, with sixteen percent more guests walking in. The next quarter, up twenty-one percent, with thirteen percent more guests. That was while restaurant operators kept reporting fewer customers, month after month after month. [PAUSE]
[VISUAL: menu with 3 for Me small in the corner, the rest of the menu big]
Now, Chili's is not a charity. This year the growth cooled off, and they've been raising prices too. About one in five guests eats off the Three for Me menu, and only about one in eight picks the ten ninety-nine version. That deal is the bait. Everything else on the menu is the business.
But it's bait you can see. The number in the ad is a number you can check. And that, it turns out, is what people were starving for.
