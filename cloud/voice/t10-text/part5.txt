So here's the question. If everybody is losing customers, how did one big chain win them?

This summer, a different guy posted a photo of a different price. A Bacon King meal on a Burger King menu. Eighteen dollars and nineteen cents. His caption: why should I get this when I could just go to a restaurant?

And a restaurant answered him. Chili's. This is what we've been saying. Then: why do y'all let fast food play you like this? A chicken sandwich, fries, bottomless chips and salsa, and an unlimited drink. Ten ninety-nine. That reply got more than five million views.

Here's the twist. While other places were hiding money in the fine print, Chili's put one price in giant letters and dared you to compare. Three for Me. Ten ninety-nine. It wasn't an accident. Over the decade before, the big fast food chains had raised their prices by about sixty percent on average, according to one analysis. So in 2024, Chili's picked a fight. A new burger that Chili's says has twice the beef of a Big Mac, at a price a drive-thru couldn't match. They even ran an ad where a pepper mascot bangs on the window, begging for a job. Because, the joke goes, Chili's doesn't pay for mascots.

And the customers came. From April to June of 2025, sales at existing Chili's jumped twenty-four percent, with sixteen percent more guests walking in. The next quarter, up twenty-one percent, with thirteen percent more guests. That was while restaurant operators kept reporting fewer customers, month after month after month. [PAUSE]

Now, Chili's is not a charity. This year the growth cooled off, and they've been raising prices too. About one in five guests eats off the Three for Me menu, and only about one in eight picks the ten ninety-nine version. That deal is the bait. Everything else on the menu is the business.

But it's bait you can see. The number in the ad is a number you can check. And that, it turns out, is what people were starving for.
