Anderson's letter went to the office of Butler County's auditor, Roger Reynolds. His office runs the weights and measures inspectors. The people who check that a gallon is really a gallon.

Here's the rule they test against. Scan a hundred items, and a store is allowed two mistakes. A third one, and it fails.

In October twenty twenty-two, they walked into every Dollar General in the county. All twenty stores.

Every single one failed. [PAUSE]

The best store was wrong on almost seventeen percent of the items they checked. The worst one, on Eaton Road in Hamilton, had seventeen items checked. Fifteen rang up different from the shelf.

Eighty-eight percent. In that store, a price tag was right about one time in eight.

A six pack of Diet Coke, four dollars on the shelf, five twenty-five at checkout. A Snapple, one dollar on the shelf and a dollar fifty at the register. In a store with the word dollar in its name.

To be fair, one store in the county made its mistakes in the customer's favor. One.

Ohio's attorney general sued. In his words, this seems like a company trying to make an extra buck and hoping no one will notice. Dollar General settled for one million dollars and agreed to fix its prices.

So how does a price tag end up this wrong in the first place?
