So how does unlimited steak for under twenty bucks make money? Who's covering Dale? And why is the real threat to this place not the guy with four plates, but an empty chair? Five rounds. Dale versus the house. The house is Golden Corral. The biggest buffet chain in America. About three hundred fifty restaurants, around a hundred fifty items on the line, and a dinner that runs roughly seventeen to twenty-one dollars, depending on the town. The man running it has worked there for forty-one years. He has met every kind of Dale there is. And on that line, as many times as you ask: sirloin steak. Now walk into a grocery store. This July, sirloin averaged fourteen dollars and fifty-nine cents a pound. That's the government's number. So Dale's math is simple. Two pounds of steak is twenty-nine dollars at the store. He paid nineteen. In Dale's head, everything after pound one is theft. Legal, delicious theft. And honestly? On steak alone, Dale might be right. A restaurant buys cheaper than you do, but two pounds of sirloin is still real money. Round one, Dale. Enjoy it, big guy. But Dale has to walk to the steak. And that walk is not an accident. Golden Corral's CEO told The Wall Street Journal the buffet is built in what he calls modules. Every module pairs something expensive with something cheap. Shrimp sits with hush puppies and french fries. The Journal's own example: meatloaf, flanked by potatoes, gravy and green beans. Nothing is hidden. It's just arranged. Translation from corporate: nothing expensive is ever left alone without a chaperone made of potato. The Hustle once priced out a buffet plate with wholesale data. A serving of steak: about two dollars and twenty-five cents. A serving of potatoes: thirty cents. Beef has only gotten pricier since. Potatoes are still potatoes. So every scoop of mashed potatoes Dale adds, just as a side, swaps a two-dollar bite for a thirty-cent one. And nobody makes him do it. He does it to himself. With a ladle. Smiling. Then there's the roll. Warm, shiny, basically a pillow with butter on it. It's one of the cheapest things on the line, and it takes up exactly the space Dale was saving for steak number three. And here's a twist I didn't expect. At a buffet, bad food is expensive. If a dish is dry, you don't send it back. You dump it and grab something else, and the house pays twice. So the cheap stuff actually has to be good. Those potatoes are a weapon. They have to taste like one. Round two goes to the house. This year, the steak started fighting back. Ground beef hit record prices, and steak wasn't far behind. For a restaurant that promises all the sirloin you can eat, that's not a headline. That's a leak in the boat. So right next to the steak, a new neighbor moved in. A bone-in fried pork chop. Golden, crunchy, the size of a catcher's mitt. The CEO says they refuse to cheapen the menu the way other buffets did. So they didn't remove the steak. They surrounded it. According to the Journal, that pork chop costs the chain about forty percent of what the sirloin does. Nobody took Dale's steak away. They just gave him a reason to cheat on it. Every pork chop he grabs, just to try it, is sixty percent off. For them. And then, the drink. The food is unlimited. The soda is extra. A fountain drink costs a restaurant pennies. The Hustle put it around twelve cents a fill. And Dale needs something to wash down two pounds of beef. Round three, house. Dale is still smiling. Dale has not looked at the scoreboard. Quick thing. If you like knowing where your money actually goes, subscribe. It's free. Unlike the soda. Still, let's give Dale his moment. He pushes through. Four plates. Steak on every single one. Say he eats twenty-five dollars' worth of food on a nineteen-dollar ticket. He beat the house.
