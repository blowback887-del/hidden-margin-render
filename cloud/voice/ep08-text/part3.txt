The industry has a word for it: a derivative. A model built for the sale. Same logo, same size, looks identical on the shelf. But a slightly different model number, and slightly less TV behind the screen. This isn't a conspiracy theory. Back in twenty fifteen, NBC News and VentureBeat both flagged examples. Best Buy's big deal was a sixty-inch Samsung for eight hundred dollars. "Regularly" fifteen hundred. One problem. VentureBeat searched that model number. It didn't exist anywhere else. Only at Best Buy. Regularly fifteen hundred where? In whose dreams? Same year, a fifty-eight-inch Vizio made for the holiday had ten dimming zones behind the screen. Its regular cousins had sixteen or thirty-two. A slower panel than the bigger models, too. And it was still, technically, four K. What gets trimmed is whatever doesn't fit on a price tag. An HDMI port here, some brightness there. Stuff you only notice in March, in a dark room, watching a movie that now looks like it was filmed through a sock. And the odd model number isn't an accident. It's the whole trick. A TV sold nowhere else has no price history, so "regular price" means whatever the store wants it to mean. And price matching usually covers only the identical model. There is no identical model. Checkmate, Dave. Now, to be fair. Plenty of Black Friday TVs are honest deals on good sets. But the one on the front page, the one Dave is camping for, is usually doing a different job. Because here's the second layer. Read the fine print under that front-page TV. Ads have carried lines like this one. "Minimum five per store." Five. Dave is ninth in line.
