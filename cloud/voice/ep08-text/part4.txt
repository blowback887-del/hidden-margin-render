Retail calls this a doorbuster, which is a friendlier way of saying bait. The store may make nothing on that TV. It might even lose a little. In twenty eighteen, an electronics analyst at NPD told the Star Tribune who actually eats that loss. Quote: "Manufacturers pay for most of the loss." And each store only gets a limited number, so the loss is capped. What the store bought with that money is Dave. Inside the building, at dawn, wallet out, adrenaline up. Years ago, Best Buy even set up a number system for its limited doorbusters, to cut down the chaos. When a sale needs crowd control, it's not a sale. It's a show. So Dave doesn't get the doorbuster. But there's a nicer one right next to it for a hundred fifty more. And he'll need a wall mount. A soundbar, because thin TVs sound like a phone in a coffee mug. An HDMI cable with gold on it, for reasons nobody can explain. And a protection plan. A retail consultant in that same article said it out loud. High-margin accessories make up for the low-margin TV. Translation from corporate: the TV is the free bread. The soundbar is the steak. And it works at scale. Last year a record two hundred three million Americans shopped over Thanksgiving weekend. Eighty million of them walked into a store on Black Friday itself. Nobody builds a holiday that big to lose money on televisions. Quick thing. In Britain, a consumer group called Which? does something beautifully petty. They tracked a full year of prices around Black Friday. The latest batch: a hundred seventy-five products, eight big retailers. Eighty-three percent were the same price, or cheaper, at some other time of year. And on Black Friday itself, not one of those deals was at its cheapest price of the year. Not one.
