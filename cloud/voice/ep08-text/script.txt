## CHAPTER 1 — The TV That Didn't Exist in September (0:25–2:45)

VO: Imagine a guy named Dave. It's Black Friday, five a.m., and Dave is in a folding chair outside a big-box store, holding a newspaper ad like it's a treasure map. Front page: a huge name-brand TV, half off.
SEE: Stock: night line outside a store, people in coats, breath steam. Photo of a sale flyer, slow push-in. Caption "DAVE (not his real name. not a real guy.)".

VO: Here's what Dave doesn't know. The TV on that ad did not exist in September. [PAUSE]
SEE: Calendar flips back Nov → Sep; the TV on the shelf fades to an empty outline with "?". Half-second silence, then hit.

VO: The industry has a word for it: a derivative. A model built for the sale. Same logo, same size, looks identical on the shelf. But a slightly different model number, and slightly less TV behind the screen.
SEE: Split screen: two identical TVs. Word "DERIVATIVE" appears by words. Zoom to the model sticker: one extra letter glows yellow (the TALISMAN of the episode).

VO: This isn't a conspiracy theory. Back in twenty fifteen, NBC News and VentureBeat both flagged examples. Best Buy's big deal was a sixty-inch Samsung for eight hundred dollars. "Regularly" fifteen hundred.
SEE: Archive-style card "2015". Tag: $1,499.99 crossed → $799.99. Source line small: NBC News / VentureBeat, Nov 2015.

VO: One problem. VentureBeat searched that model number. It didn't exist anywhere else. Only at Best Buy.
SEE: Quote card (VentureBeat, Nov 24, 2015): "it doesn't exist anywhere else". Our graphic: a store shelf with one lonely model sticker. GAG: tumbleweed.

VO: Regularly fifteen hundred where? In whose dreams?
SEE: GAG — stock: man squinting suspiciously at a price tag, dramatic zoom.

VO: Same year, a fifty-eight-inch Vizio made for the holiday had ten dimming zones behind the screen. Its regular cousins had sixteen or thirty-two. A slower panel than the bigger models, too. And it was still, technically, four K.
SEE: X-ray of the back of two TVs: grid of 32 light zones vs 10 big ones. Numbers 10 / 16 / 32 with icons. "60Hz vs 120Hz".

VO: What gets trimmed is whatever doesn't fit on a price tag. An HDMI port here, some brightness there. Stuff you only notice in March, in a dark room, watching a movie that now looks like it was filmed through a sock.
SEE: Ports on the back: one fades out. Then GAG — stock: couple squinting at a murky TV in a dark living room; caption "4K (of fog)".

VO: And the odd model number isn't an accident. It's the whole trick. A TV sold nowhere else has no price history, so "regular price" means whatever the store wants it to mean. And price matching usually covers only the identical model. There is no identical model. Checkmate, Dave.
SEE: Price-history graph that is just a blank line with a shrug. "PRICE MATCH" stamp → "DENIED". Chess piece tips over.

VO: Now, to be fair. Plenty of Black Friday TVs are honest deals on good sets. But the one on the front page, the one Dave is camping for, is usually doing a different job.
SEE: Stock: happy family unboxing a TV (honest deal). Back to the flyer front page. THROUGH-MAP: sticker layer 1 peels — "1. A DIFFERENT TV" filled; layers 2 and 3 still "?".

## CHAPTER 2 — The Store Isn't Selling TVs (2:45–4:40)

VO: Because here's the second layer. Read the fine print under that front-page TV. Ads have carried lines like this one. "Minimum five per store."
SEE: Magnifier slides over tiny flyer text (our recreated flyer, labelled "ad wording reported by CNNMoney, 2009"); red circle + arrow on "min. 5 per store". Footnote stays ≥ 2.5 s.

VO: Five. Dave is ninth in line. [PAUSE]
SEE: Stock: long line; counter ticks 1…9 over heads; freeze on #9. Record scratch.

VO: Retail calls this a doorbuster, which is a friendlier way of saying bait. The store may make nothing on that TV. It might even lose a little.
SEE: Word DOORBUSTER → flips to BAIT (corporate-translation subtitle gag). Fishing hook lowers a tiny TV.

VO: In twenty eighteen, an electronics analyst at NPD told the Star Tribune who actually eats that loss. Quote: "Manufacturers pay for most of the loss." And each store only gets a limited number, so the loss is capped.
SEE: Quote card with source: Stephen Baker, NPD — Star Tribune, Nov 28, 2018. Coins moving from "MAKER" to "STORE".

VO: What the store bought with that money is Dave. Inside the building, at dawn, wallet out, adrenaline up.

VO: Years ago, Best Buy even set up a number system for its limited doorbusters, to cut down the chaos. When a sale needs crowd control, it's not a sale. It's a show.
SEE: Numbers "#1 … #5" popping over heads in a queue (our graphic over stock). Caption: "now serving: nobody past 5".

VO: So Dave doesn't get the doorbuster. But there's a nicer one right next to it for a hundred fifty more. And he'll need a wall mount. A soundbar, because thin TVs sound like a phone in a coffee mug. An HDMI cable with gold on it, for reasons nobody can explain. And a protection plan.
SEE: Picture-by-word: cart fills item by item, each with a cash-register ding and its own sound. Running total climbs. GAG: phone dropped in a mug.

VO: A retail consultant in that same article said it out loud. High-margin accessories make up for the low-margin TV.

VO: Translation from corporate: the TV is the free bread. The soundbar is the steak.
SEE: GAG — stock: bread basket on a restaurant table → steak. Subtitle "translated from Corporate".

VO: And it works at scale. Last year a record two hundred three million Americans shopped over Thanksgiving weekend. Eighty million of them walked into a store on Black Friday itself. Nobody builds a holiday that big to lose money on televisions.
SEE: US map filling with dots; "202.9M" then "80.3M in stores" (NRF, Dec 2025). Stock: packed mall from above. THROUGH-MAP: layer 2 peels — "2. THE TV IS BAIT".

## CHAPTER 3 — The Discount That's There All Year (4:40–5:50)

## CHAPTER 4 — The TV That Pays Rent (5:50–8:40)

VO: But none of that explains the two hundred twenty-eight dollar TV. A real sixty-five inches, from a real brand, stacked to the ceiling. How does anybody build that and survive?
SEE: Return to the opening TV and the $228 tag. Stock: pallets of boxed TVs in a warehouse store. Music changes — the "big one".

VO: For that, forget the store. Read the TV company's own paperwork.
SEE: Document slides in: FORM 10-K, SEC. Paper sound. 3D moment: glossy TV rotates, back panel opens like a safe.

VO: First, the boring half of the answer. Screens got cheap to make. An industry analyst put it this way in twenty eighteen: the price of a thirty-two-inch in two thousand eight buys you a sixty-inch now. Factories did that part.
SEE: Two TVs growing 32" → 60" under the same price tag "<$400" (Paul Gagnon, IHS Markit — Star Tribune 2018). Stock: panel factory robots.

VO: But a factory can only cut so far. Somebody still has to want to sell it at a loss. So here's the fun half.
SEE: Price bar sliding down, stops at a floor line "cost", then pushes below it — coral red. Question mark.

VO: Vizio. Twenty twenty-three. They sold over a billion dollars' worth of TVs and soundbars. Gross profit on all that hardware? Negative eight point six million.
SEE: Line from the filing highlighted: Device net revenue $1,081.8M → Device gross profit $(8.6)M in coral red.

VO: They lost money making the thing their name is on.
SEE: GAG — stock: man in suit, slow facepalm.

VO: Same year, same company, a division called Platform Plus earned three hundred sixty-five million dollars in gross profit. Platform Plus is ads. The home screen. The free channels. And licensing data about what people watch.
SEE: Second line highlights in green: Platform+ gross profit $364.9M. Icons appear by word: ad, home screen, free channels, data.

VO: Read those two lines again. The TV business lost money. The watching-you-watch-TV business made all of the gross profit.
SEE: Two bars side by side: tiny red below zero, tall green. Hold 4–5 s with slow push (main reveal).

VO: Roku puts the quiet part in its annual report. It says it may run a negative margin on devices, on purpose, to grow its user base. In twenty twenty-four it lost about eighty million dollars on devices, and made almost one point nine billion on the platform.
SEE: Quote strip from Roku 10-K. Numbers −$80M / +$1.9B with device and screen icons.

VO: How does a TV know what you're watching? Software called ACR. Automatic content recognition. It identifies what's on the screen, from any input. Cable. Game console. That one DVD you still own.
SEE: Screen pixels sampled → matched to a library (simple animation). Icons by word. GAG: dusty DVD case.

VO: In twenty seventeen, Vizio paid two point two million dollars to settle with the FTC and New Jersey, which said eleven million TVs had been tracking what people watched, second by second, without them knowing.
SEE: FTC press release card, Feb 6, 2017. "11,000,000 TVs". Stock: living room, TV glow on a couch — lens icon in the corner of the TV.

VO: These days Vizio has to ask permission first. It's on one of those setup screens you speedrun with the OK button.
SEE: GAG — hand hammering OK on a remote; screens "I AGREE" flying past; meme caption "me reading the terms".

VO: Now do the math Vizio's investors did. Each active account brought in about thirty-two dollars a year in revenue. Keep the TV seven years, and that's two hundred twenty-four dollars. The TV cost two twenty-eight. [PAUSE]

VO: The price tag isn't what the TV costs. It's the down payment. You pay the rest in attention.
SEE: Tag splits: "$228 now" + "the rest: your evenings". Stock: family watching TV, faces lit.

VO: That's why even the cheap ones are smart TVs now. A dumb TV just sits there, being a TV, earning nothing. Like a freeloader.
