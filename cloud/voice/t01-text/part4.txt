Chapter three. The screen.

The waiter brings the card reader and flips it around. Three big buttons. Twenty. Twenty-five. Thirty. And one tiny button that says no tip, hiding in the corner like it's ashamed of itself.

That's not an accident. Economists studied more than thirteen million New York taxi rides. When the screen showed twenty, twenty-five and thirty instead of fifteen, twenty and twenty-five, tips went up by roughly ten percent. Same rides. Same city. Different buttons. More people also tipped nothing at all. Push too hard, and some people just walk.

And there's a second switch. Toast, a major restaurant payment system, lets the restaurant pick whether the suggested tip is figured before tax or after tax.

Etiquette experts say you tip on the food, before tax. The screen can quietly take its percent of everything. The tax. The wellness fee. Even the service charge.

Let's do Jake's math. Food, eighty. Wellness fee, two eighty. Service charge, sixteen. Tax, about eight. That's a hundred and six eighty before the tip. Jake taps twenty percent. Twenty-one thirty-six. Twenty percent of the food would be sixteen. So the screen charged Jake an extra five bucks for pressing the button he always presses.

Back in March, diners noticed suggested tips at a Dave and Buster's reportedly being figured on the total after tax. One restaurant owner called that double dipping. A restaurant owner. Even she thinks it's double dipping.

Some receipts go further. Automatic gratuity already added, and below it, an empty line for a tip. Just sitting there. Hoping. That's how you tip twice.

And the QR code on your receipt? A class action filed this January says one of those pay by phone apps adds a convenience fee at the very last step. Convenient for who?

Ordering online is the same game. Shake Shack got sued in California over service and delivery fees that, according to the lawsuit, only showed up at the very last step. You find out the real price when your thumb is already on the button.

People are catching on. In a twenty twenty-five Bankrate survey, thirty-eight percent of Americans said those preset tip screens annoy them. And more than a quarter said a screen like that makes them tip less, or nothing.

So, the final bill. Jake ordered eighty dollars of food. He paid a hundred and twenty-eight sixteen. Sixty percent over the menu. And thirty-seven dollars of it was some kind of tip. Almost half the price of the food.
