In Seattle, a celebrity chef's restaurant group swapped tipping for a twenty percent service charge. Customers figured that was the tip. According to the lawsuit, servers got only about fourteen percent of it, on average. And nobody explained that to the customers. The company says it did nothing wrong. It still settled, in twenty nineteen, for two point four million dollars, paid to more than a thousand workers.

In New York, Per Se, one of the fanciest restaurants in America, added twenty percent to private dinners. The state said that money went to the restaurant, not the waiters, and guests weren't clearly told it wasn't a gratuity. Five hundred thousand dollars to settle with the state.

And in San Francisco, a Hilton fought its own banquet servers in court for twelve years over service charges. Then it agreed to pay twelve million, in a proposed settlement.

The part that really stings? One server who sued a Los Angeles restaurant group said about eight in ten customers didn't tip, because they thought the service charge was the tip. So the waiter loses the tip. The owner keeps the charge. And you walk out feeling generous. Everybody loses, except the guy with the keys.
