So, yes. One of the most beloved characters of Christmas started as a store promotion. Which might be the most American sentence I've ever said. About twenty years later, Philadelphia police named the day after Thanksgiving "Black Friday." Because of the traffic. Officers hated it. Merchants tried to rebrand it "Big Friday." That went nowhere. So the name got a makeover instead: "black," as in profit. In the black. Nice story. Just not the original one. Here's the thing. We think of Christmas as an old tradition that business took over later. But a lot of the magic was built on purpose, by people selling things. And it worked. Fast forward to today. On average, November and December alone bring in about nineteen percent of America's entire year of retail sales. Almost a fifth of the year, packed into its darkest weeks. To pull it off, Amazon plans to hire more than three hundred thousand people this season. That's up twenty percent from last year. UPS wants about a hundred thousand. And the delivery companies charge extra for the rush. Last season, holiday "peak surcharges" were added per package, from the fall into the middle of January. Even the delivery gets a Christmas markup. Even the soundtrack is a business. One Christmas song from nineteen ninety-four still earns its singer an estimated two and a half to three million dollars a year. Some people's Christmas never really ends. But the most interesting money isn't made on the stuff in the boxes. It's made on a small piece of plastic. Gift cards are the second most popular present Americans plan to give, right after clothes. Last season, shoppers planned to spend twenty-nine billion dollars on them. Now look at a gift card from the company's side. You hand over fifty dollars today. The company gets your cash today. It owes you a coffee... someday. Accountants call that "deferred revenue." Translation: an interest-free loan, from you, to a corporation. And sometimes, someday never comes. Starbucks cards in the U.S. don't expire. But the company knows, statistically, how much money will never be spent. And it books that as revenue. It's called "breakage." In fiscal twenty twenty-five, in its company-operated stores alone, Starbucks recognized more than two hundred million dollars of it.
