In twenty twenty-four, the Federal Trade Commission ordered eight companies to explain the pricing tools they sell. Not stores. The middlemen. Names like Mastercard, JPMorgan Chase, Accenture and McKinsey.

In January twenty twenty-five, the FTC published its first findings. The data these tools can use to set a price includes your location, your demographics, your browsing history, the things you leave sitting in your cart, and even how your mouse moves across the page. [PAUSE]

The FTC couldn't name real cases, so it gave hypothetical ones. A new parent, for example, could be shown higher priced baby thermometers first. The theory being, a tired parent at two in the morning isn't comparison shopping.

The agency said the study wasn't a law enforcement case. But it found these middlemen worked with at least two hundred fifty clients, from grocery stores to clothing retailers.

Then the idea went airborne. In twenty twenty-five, Delta said it was using AI from a company called Fetcherr to price a small slice of its flights, with a goal of twenty percent of its domestic network by the end of that year.

Three senators wrote that this would likely push fares up to each traveler's personal pain point. Delta wrote back. Its ticket pricing, it said, never takes personal data into account.

And now the same question has walked into your local store. Walmart says digital price tags will be on the shelves of all its U.S. stores within the next year. A worker used to swap paper tags by hand. Now a price can change instantly from a central computer. [PAUSE]

Walmart says the tags have no cameras or microphones. And its CEO wrote to customers that Walmart prices the product, not the person.

That's the promise. But the tag itself will never tell you what's behind the number on it. So people stopped waiting for promises. And started writing laws.
