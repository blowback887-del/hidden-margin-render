# T05 — Your Neighbor Paid Less for the Same Groceries — сценарий (версия 1, после редактуры и anti-ai-writing)
Окраска: тихий техно-триллер («цена смотрит на тебя») + сухая сатира как приправа. Компании не обвиняем: факты расследований и их собственные ответы.
Сквозная линия: эксперимент с одинаковыми корзинами (Сиэтл, Вашингтон) → откуда это взялось → кто продаёт инструменты → ценник в магазине → люди дают отпор.
Предмет-талисман: дюжина яиц с пятью ценами (возвращается в финале).
Строки `> SEE:` — что видим (не озвучиваются).

## PART 1

One dozen eggs. Same store. Same time. Five different prices. [PAUSE]
> SEE: коробка яиц крупно; над ней по очереди всплывают пять ценников $3.99, $4.28, $4.59, $4.69, $4.79

Three ninety-nine. Four twenty-eight. Four fifty-nine. Four sixty-nine. Four seventy-nine.
> SEE: скриншот отчёта Consumer Reports с ценами яиц, маркер

That was a Safeway in Washington, D.C., shopped through the Instacart app. Five shoppers, one carton, five prices. And nobody told them.
> SEE: пять телефонов в разных руках, на каждом свой ценник

In Seattle, thirty-nine people put the exact same twenty items in their cart, from the same store, at the same time. They got five different totals. The cheapest was one hundred fourteen dollars and thirty-four cents. The most expensive, one twenty-three ninety-three. [PAUSE]
> SEE: сетка из 39 корзин; пять итогов подсвечиваются; $114.34 и $123.93 крупно

Same cart. Same store. Somebody paid nearly ten dollars more for nothing.
> SEE: две одинаковые корзины, на одной +$9.59

So who decides who that somebody is? And is the price tag at your store next?
> SEE: электронный ценник на полке, на нём мигает знак вопроса

## PART 2

Those numbers come from an investigation by Consumer Reports, the Groundwork Collaborative and More Perfect Union. Last year they recruited more than four hundred volunteers in four cities. Same items, same stores, same moment.
> SEE: скриншот пресс-релиза Consumer Reports; карта: North Canton, Saint Paul, Seattle, Washington D.C.

Nearly three quarters of the items came up at different prices for different people. Some had five prices at once. On staples like cereal, pasta and peanut butter, the gap averaged thirteen percent. A box of Wheat Thins? Up to twenty-three. [PAUSE]
> SEE: столбики цен; коробка крекеров с подписью «+23%»

The researchers found these tests at stores like Safeway, Kroger and Costco, running through Instacart's app. Instacart's own AI pricing software, called Eversight, made them possible.
> SEE: ряд вывесок магазинов; слово Eversight на экране

And here's my favorite detail. On some sale items, the sale price stayed the same for everyone. What changed was the "original" price above it. So one shopper saw a small discount and another saw a bigger one. On the exact same sale. [PAUSE]
> SEE: два ценника кетчупа SALE $4.99; зачёркнутая цена над ними разная — $5.29 и $5.59 (пример Groundwork, Вашингтон, 10 сент. 2025)

Instacart said the tests were random, didn't use personal data, and the differences were negligible. The report did the math anyway. Spread across a year of groceries for a family of four, it estimated about twelve hundred dollars.
> SEE: «negligible» в кавычках; счётчик до $1,200 за год

Instacart also said the shoppers didn't know they were in an experiment. Which, if you think about it, is kind of the point of an experiment. Nobody tells the lab rat.
> SEE: мышь в лабиринте с тележкой (мем)

Which means a price tag isn't what you think it is. It used to be a fact about the product. Here, it's a test. How much will people pay before they close the app? [PAUSE]
> SEE: бумажный ценник превращается в электронный; на нём текст «HOW MUCH WILL YOU PAY?»

That December, right after the report came out, Instacart said it would end all of its item price tests.
> SEE: скриншот обновления Consumer Reports от 22 декабря 2025

Problem solved? For one app, maybe. Those tests were random. The bigger question is what happens when a price isn't random at all. And that idea is a lot older than the app.
> SEE: календарь отматывается назад к 2012

## PART 3

Twenty twelve. The Wall Street Journal tests Staples dot com from more than forty-two thousand ZIP codes. A plain Swingline stapler.
> SEE: степлер на белом фоне; карта США из точек

Some people saw fourteen twenty-nine. Others saw fifteen seventy-nine. The pattern tracked one thing above all. Whether a rival store, like OfficeMax or Office Depot, was within about twenty miles.
> SEE: две цены рядом; круг радиусом 20 миль вокруг магазина-конкурента

Live near competition, get the discount. Live where Staples was the only game in town, pay full price. And the Journal noticed something awkward. The discounts tended to land in richer ZIP codes. The higher prices landed where people earned less.
> SEE: две карты районов; подписи среднего дохода ~$59,900 и ~$48,700

Staples said prices vary by geography for "a variety of factors," including the costs of doing business.
> SEE: цитата на экране

Same year, Orbitz. The travel site found that Mac users spent twenty to thirty dollars more a night on hotels. So it started showing them pricier hotels more prominently. To be fair, Orbitz said it never charged them more for the same room. It just assumed your laptop knew your budget. [PAUSE]
> SEE: MacBook и обычный ноутбук; на экранах разные списки отелей

Then, twenty nineteen. A Minneapolis TV station, KARE 11, gets a tip from a viewer. She bought an electric shaver inside Target for ninety-nine ninety-nine. Back in her car, the Target app showed sixty-nine ninety-nine.
> SEE: парковка Target; телефон с приложением; $99.99 → $69.99

So reporters spent two months testing it. In one test, the app showed a Samsung TV for four ninety-nine ninety-nine. They pulled into the store's parking lot, and the same TV in the same app jumped to five ninety-nine ninety-nine.
> SEE: машина въезжает на парковку; цена ТВ на экране прыгает на $100

Out of ten items, four went up. The total difference was two hundred sixty-two dollars. So the cheapest way to shop at Target was to not be at Target. [PAUSE]
> SEE: машина уезжает с парковки; табличка у въезда «PRICES GO UP HERE»

Target said the app simply switched to in-store prices once you were in the store, and it updated the app to label which price was which. But think about what the app had to know to do that. Where your phone was. Close enough to know you'd pulled in.
> SEE: точка геолокации на карте, приближение к парковке

See the pattern? Every time someone gets caught, there's an explanation and a fix. Meanwhile the tools keep getting better. Who's building them?
> SEE: экран гаснет, остаётся мигающий курсор

## PART 4

In twenty twenty-four, the Federal Trade Commission ordered eight companies to explain the pricing tools they sell. Not stores. The middlemen. Names like Mastercard, JPMorgan Chase, Accenture and McKinsey.
> SEE: скриншот пресс-релиза FTC; восемь названий выстраиваются в столбик

In January twenty twenty-five, the FTC published its first findings. The data these tools can use to set a price includes your location, your demographics, your browsing history, the things you leave sitting in your cart, and even how your mouse moves across the page. [PAUSE]
> SEE: курсор медленно движется по сайту, за ним тянется след точек

The FTC couldn't name real cases, so it gave hypothetical ones. A new parent, for example, could be shown higher priced baby thermometers first. The theory being, a tired parent at two in the morning isn't comparison shopping.
> SEE: детская бутылочка в корзине; часы 2:00 AM

The agency said the study wasn't a law enforcement case. But it found these middlemen worked with at least two hundred fifty clients, from grocery stores to clothing retailers.
> SEE: скриншот пресс-релиза FTC с маркером «at least 250 clients»

If you've found something useful so far, subscribe. Every week we take apart one more way prices get rigged. And unlike your cart, it's the same price for everyone.
> SEE: кнопка подписки, ценник «$0 — same for everyone»

Then the idea went airborne. In twenty twenty-five, Delta said it was using AI from a company called Fetcherr to price a small slice of its flights, with a goal of twenty percent of its domestic network by the end of that year.
> SEE: самолёт Delta на рулёжке; график 3% → 20%

Three senators wrote that this would likely push fares up to each traveler's personal pain point. Delta wrote back. Its ticket pricing, it said, never takes personal data into account.
> SEE: цитата сенаторов; цитата ответа Delta

And now the same question has walked into your local store. Walmart says digital price tags will be on the shelves of all its U.S. stores within the next year. A worker used to swap paper tags by hand. Now a price can change instantly from a central computer. [PAUSE]
> SEE: работник меняет бумажные ценники → ряд электронных ценников меняет цифры одновременно

Walmart says the tags have no cameras or microphones. And its CEO wrote to customers that Walmart prices the product, not the person.
> SEE: скриншот письма, маркер на «We price the product, not the person»

That's the promise. But the tag itself will never tell you what's behind the number on it. So people stopped waiting for promises. And started writing laws.
> SEE: ценник крупно, цифры мерцают; склейка — здание законодательного собрания

## PART 5

New York went first. Since November twenty twenty-five, if a business there uses your personal data to set your price, it has to tell you. In these exact words. This price was set by an algorithm using your personal data. [PAUSE]
> SEE: экран доставки еды; внизу плашка «THIS PRICE WAS SET BY AN ALGORITHM USING YOUR PERSONAL DATA»

Imagine seeing that under your milk. And this summer, New York lawmakers voted to go further, with an outright ban. It's waiting on the governor's signature.
> SEE: та же плашка под пакетом молока; склейка — здание Капитолия штата Нью-Йорк

Then Maryland. Since October first, grocery stores and delivery apps there can't use your personal data to raise what you pay for food.
> SEE: карта, Мэриленд подсвечен

And this fall, Seattle became the first city in America to ban it. The list of things grocers can't use to set your price is a portrait of modern life. Your race. Your gender. Whether you have a job. Your browsing and your social media. And your conversations with chatbots. [PAUSE]
> SEE: список появляется построчно; последняя строка крупно

Somebody, somewhere, had to write down that the price of your groceries shouldn't depend on what you told an AI last night.
> SEE: телефон в темноте с окном чата

Not everyone cheered. Grocers warned the ban could cost shoppers coupons and loyalty discounts. And Safeway said it doesn't use personal information to charge customers more.
> SEE: заявление отраслевой ассоциации; цитата Safeway

Seattle's rules don't start until twenty twenty-seven. And the FTC proposed new guidance this August. Until any of that reaches your store, the only defense is you.
> SEE: календарь с пометкой 2027

## PART 6

So how do you make the algorithm guess wrong?
> SEE: кубик-рубика крутится и собирается

Check the price twice. Once in the app, logged in. Once in a private browser window, logged out. If they don't match, now you know.
> SEE: два экрана рядом, разные цены

Turn off location for store apps you don't need it in. Remember that Target parking lot.
> SEE: переключатель Location → Off

Don't trust the crossed-out price. Trust the price you actually pay. On the same sale, the "original" price was the part that moved.
> SEE: зачёркнутая цена отваливается со ценника

And if you're in New York, look for that one sentence. If it's there, you're being priced personally.
> SEE: плашка-раскрытие крупно

Back to those eggs. Five shoppers, one carton, five prices. The eggs didn't change. The only thing that changed was the shopper. [PAUSE]
> SEE: та же коробка яиц; пять ценников снова над ней

So next time you see a price, ask one question. Is this the price of the eggs, or the price of me?
> SEE: ценник медленно поворачивается к камере
