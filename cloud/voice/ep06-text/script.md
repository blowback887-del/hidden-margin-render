## CHAPTER 1 — THE DIME
VIS: chapter card "Chapter 1 — The Dime".
EN: Meet Mike. Mike is made up, but his numbers aren't. He owns one gas station off a highway in Ohio.
VIS: live: small roadside station at dawn. Caption "Mike (imaginary). Numbers: real."
EN: His sign says four dollars and seventeen cents. That's about the national average as November started.
VIS: price sign; corner chip "AAA, Nov 1, 2026: $4.17".
EN: Mike looks rich. Mike is not rich.
VIS: GAG: meme caption over sad man counting coins "Me 'owning' a gas station".
EN: Let's cut one gallon open and see who eats.
VIS: CENTRAL DEVICE: the "gallon cutter" — a giant gallon jug slides onto a cutting board; slices fall out one by one. Running map: "Where your gallon goes" bar.
EN: The industry's own trade group did this math with a three-dollar gallon, so we'll use it.
VIS: chip "NACS example: $3.11 gallon".
EN: More than half of it is crude oil. About a dollar eighty-five.
VIS: slice 1: oil barrel, "$1.85".
EN: The refinery takes about forty cents.
VIS: slice 2: refinery stock footage, "40¢".
EN: Taxes take about fifty more. Federal tax alone is eighteen point four cents, and Congress hasn't raised it since nineteen ninety-three.
VIS: slice 3: "≈51¢ taxes", "federal 18.4¢ — not raised since 1993".
EN: What's left for Mike is his markup: about thirty-five cents a gallon.
VIS: last slice glows yellow "35¢ — Mike".
EN: Thirty-five cents! Mike's getting a boat.
VIS: GAG: stock photo of a yacht, caption "Mike's boat (pending)".
EN: Not so fast. Out of those thirty-five cents, Mike pays a truck to haul the fuel in. Six cents. Then credit card fees. About eight cents a gallon.
VIS: tanker truck "−6¢ delivery"; card swipe close-up "−8.4¢ card fees (2023)"; the 35¢ slice shrinks.
EN: So on average, the card companies get more than eight cents a gallon. Without owning a pump, a tank, or a mop.
VIS: GAG: "translation from corporate" subtitle: "Processing fee = we exist."
EN: Across the industry last year, card fees hit a record twenty-one billion dollars. About four and a half billion of that was charged on the taxes. Fees. On taxes. That stations just pass along to the government.
VIS: counter "$21.3B card fees (2025)" → sliver "$4.6B on fuel taxes". GAG: red circle on "on taxes".
EN: Then the lights, the staff, the pumps wearing out, and the occasional guy who drives off without paying.
VIS: quick cuts: lights, worker, pump, car driving away. Counter falling.
EN: What Mike actually keeps: about thirteen cents a gallon. On a good day, a dime and change.
VIS: hard zoom on "≈13¢" with a dull thud. Running map fills: "Mike: 13¢".
EN: [PAUSE] Now do the math for your whole year.
VIS: calendar flipping, odometer.
EN: The average car burns more than five hundred gallons a year. At thirteen cents, that's about sixty-five dollars of profit for Mike. From every fill-up you make. All year. Combined.
VIS: authorial calc: "500 gal × 13¢ ≈ $65 / year".
EN: At early-November prices, sixty-five dollars buys about one tank. You make Mike one tank of money a year.
VIS: calc chip "$65 ÷ $4.17 ≈ 15.6 gal ≈ 1 tank".
EN: If he really made two dollars a gallon, you'd be worth a thousand dollars a year to him. You are not worth a thousand dollars a year to Mike.
VIS: two bars "$1,000 (what people think)" vs "$65 (reality)". GAG: record scratch + "Yep. That's you."
EN: So if the gallon barely pays... Mike has to squeeze the only thing he controls out there. The sign.
VIS: camera pans up to the price sign.

## CHAPTER 2 — THE SIGN
VIS: chapter card "Chapter 2 — The Sign".
EN: Look closely at any price in America. Four seventeen... and nine-tenths.
VIS: macro shot of the tiny 9/10 on a sign; red circle + arrow.
EN: Nobody in history has ever paid nine-tenths of a cent. There's no coin for it. You can't even find it under the couch.
VIS: GAG: stock photo hand searching couch cushions, caption "looking for 9/10 of a cent".
EN: It started in the nineteen-thirties. In nineteen thirty-two, Washington put a federal tax on gas: one cent a gallon.
VIS: archival sepia: 1930s gas station (Library of Congress). Chip "Revenue Act of 1932: 1¢/gal".
EN: Back then gas cost less than twenty cents. Adding a whole penny was a huge jump. So stations started pricing in fractions.
VIS: sepia sign "19¢" → "19⁹⁄₁₀¢"? (graphic only).
EN: The tax grew. The fraction stayed, and by the seventies nine-tenths was the standard. Because a price that starts with "four sixteen" just feels cheaper.
VIS: two signs side by side "$4.17" vs "$4.16⁹".
EN: And for Mike, that tiny fraction is no joke. The trade group says it can be up to a tenth of a store's profit on fuel.
VIS: 13¢ coin with a sliver glowing yellow "up to 10% of fuel profit".
EN: Iowa once banned it in the nineteen-eighties. A few years later, the ban was gone. The nine-tenths won.
VIS: map of Iowa stamped "BANNED" → stamp peels off. GAG: small trophy on "9/10".
EN: The second trick isn't really a trick. It's physics. Kind of.
VIS: transition: feather and rocket icons.
EN: This year, the national average went from two seventy-nine in January to four fifty-six in May.
VIS: line chart rising like a rocket; dates under it.
EN: When wholesale prices jump, Mike has to raise his sign fast, or he sells at a loss. While prices rocket up, his thirteen cents can drop to zero. Or below.
VIS: rocket on the chart; Mike's 13¢ coin shrinking to "0¢".
EN: But when prices fall, nobody calls Mike to complain that his gas is too cheap. So prices drift down slowly. Like a feather.
VIS: chart falling slowly; feather drifting.
EN: A federal study found retail prices went up more than four times faster than they came down. Economists literally call this "rockets and feathers."
VIS: chip "FTC study, 2010: up 4×+ faster than down".
EN: So here's a fun one. This fall, as prices came down, is exactly when station margins get wider.
VIS: chips "AAA: $4.56 (May) → $4.17 (Nov 1)"; feather landing on a dollar bill.
EN: The cheaper your gas gets, the happier Mike gets. Just slower than you.
VIS: GAG: contrast — cheerful music, Mike-type stock man smiling at falling sign.
EN: If this is changing how you look at the pump, hit subscribe. Every week we take apart one business you use without thinking about it.
VIS: subscribe chip, small, corner.
EN: But even feathers don't pay the rent. Thirteen cents can't. So where does the money come from? [PAUSE] Walk inside.
VIS: automatic doors open toward camera, bright store.

## CHAPTER 3 — INSIDE
VIS: chapter card "Chapter 3 — Inside".
EN: More than half the people who fill up also walk into the store. Fifty-seven percent.
VIS: overhead: people walking from pumps to door; "57% go inside".
EN: That walk is the whole business plan.
VIS: dotted arrow from pump to door. Running map: arrow appears "→ INSIDE".
EN: Here's last year across the whole industry. Fuel was sixty-five percent of sales... but only thirty-nine percent of the profit.
VIS: split screen: left pump "Fuel: 65% of sales", right "39% of profit" (NACS, 2025).
EN: Which means the store, everything inside, brought in about sixty-one percent of the profit.
VIS: right side flips: "Store: ≈61% of profit". Hard zoom, thud.
EN: Same station. Same customers. The part you barely notice makes most of the money.
VIS: small slice of pie glowing, big slice dim.
EN: Let that sink in. The thing on the giant sign by the highway is the side hustle. The candy aisle is the business.
VIS: highway sign → candy aisle. GAG: caption "side hustle" on pump, "CEO" on a candy bar.
EN: And the best part of the store isn't even on the shelves. It's behind the counter.
VIS: coffee machine, hot food case, roller grill.
EN: Food — coffee, fountain drinks, hot food — was about twenty-eight percent of inside sales. But almost thirty-nine percent of inside profit.
VIS: "Food: 28.5% of inside sales → 38.9% of inside profit".
EN: Think about what that means. To Mike, the gas is an ad. A giant glowing ad by the highway, that you pay for. Then you walk inside and buy the actual product.
VIS: authorial metaphor: pump wrapped as a billboard "AD", customer handing cash to the billboard.
EN: You paid for the commercial. [PAUSE]
VIS: GAG: freeze frame + stamp "PAID ADVERTISEMENT".
EN: And the ad is slowly shrinking. Government forecasters expect Americans to buy less fuel this year and next. Cars keep going farther on every gallon.
VIS: highway traffic stock clip; down arrow "Gasoline use ↓ 2026–2027 (EIA forecast)".
EN: So gas stations are quietly turning into something else. Restaurants with a really big parking lot.
VIS: station canopy at night, food counter glowing inside.
EN: Most stations stop at hot dogs and coffee. But one chain took this idea all the way. And it started with one station in a town of about twelve thousand people.
VIS: drone/stock: small Midwestern town, grain elevator.

## CHAPTER 4 — THE PIZZA
VIS: chapter card "Chapter 4 — The Pizza".
EN: Nineteen sixty-eight. Boone, Iowa. A guy named Don Lamberti buys a tiny three-bay service station.
VIS: archival-style sepia frame; text "Boone, Iowa · 1968".
EN: The idea came from his fuel supplier, Kurvin Fish. Don turns the station into a little store and names it after his friend's initials. K.C. Casey's.
VIS: letters "K.C." morph into "CASEY'S".
EN: It works. He opens another. And another. In small towns other chains didn't bother with.
VIS: Midwest map, dots spreading into small towns.
EN: Then, in nineteen eighty-four, Casey's tries something weird. Pizza. Made in the store. At a gas station.
VIS: dough slapped on a counter, oven door.
EN: That sounds like a dare. It became the plan. By the end of the next year, pizza was in eighty-five stores.
VIS: counter "stores with pizza: a few → 85".
EN: Later came a breakfast pizza. With sausage gravy. At a gas station. In Iowa, this is considered a balanced breakfast.
VIS: breakfast pizza close-up (stock, generic). GAG: caption "balanced breakfast" with a tiny food-pyramid icon.
EN: Today Casey's has about two thousand nine hundred and fifty stores in nineteen states. Nearly half of them in towns of five thousand people or fewer.
VIS: map with ~2,950 dots; chip "nearly half in towns ≤5,000".
EN: And by sales, Bank of America's analysts rank Casey's the fifth-biggest pizza chain in America. [PAUSE] A gas station.
VIS: ranking podium: 4 pizza logos as text-only names hidden; "#5 CASEY'S" with a fuel nozzle. Hard zoom, thud.
EN: Now open their annual report, and the "gas is the bait" idea stops being a theory.
VIS: 10-K document pages, highlighter.
EN: Last fiscal year, Casey's sold three and a half billion gallons of fuel and made about forty-three cents a gallon.
VIS: "3.52 billion gallons · 42.6¢/gal".
EN: On prepared food and drinks, it kept almost fifty-nine cents of every dollar, before running the store.
VIS: "Pizza & drinks: 58.6¢ of every $1".
EN: Let's make that real. Ten dollars of gas at the early-November average is about two and a half gallons. Casey's keeps about a dollar of that. Ten dollars of pizza? About six.
VIS: authorial calc split screen: "$10 gas → ~$1" vs "$10 pizza → ~$5.86".
EN: Same customer. Same ten bucks. Almost six times the money.
VIS: hard zoom "≈6×".
EN: Over the last three years, food and store items were about thirty-six percent of Casey's revenue... but sixty-three percent of its gross profit.
VIS: big donut chart filling "36% revenue → 63% profit".
EN: Last fiscal year Casey's made seven hundred and fourteen million dollars. And in April it joined the S and P five hundred. From one service station in Boone.
VIS: "$714M net income · S&P 500, April 2026". Fireworks? no — subtle gold flash.
EN: So is Casey's a gas station that sells pizza... or a pizza chain that happens to sell gas?
VIS: GAG: pizza box with a gas nozzle sticking out, caption "Parking lot: very large".
