Mike has to squeeze the only thing he controls out there. The sign. Look closely at any price in America. Four seventeen... and nine-tenths. Nobody in history has ever paid nine-tenths of a cent. There's no coin for it. You can't even find it under the couch. It started in the nineteen-thirties. In nineteen thirty-two, Washington put a federal tax on gas: one cent a gallon. Back then gas cost less than twenty cents. Adding a whole penny was a huge jump. So stations started pricing in fractions. The tax grew. The fraction stayed, and by the seventies nine-tenths was the standard. Because a price that starts with "four sixteen" just feels cheaper. And for Mike, that tiny fraction is no joke. The trade group says it can be up to a tenth of a store's profit on fuel. Iowa once banned it in the nineteen-eighties. A few years later, the ban was gone. The nine-tenths won. When wholesale prices jump, Mike has to raise his sign fast, or he sells at a loss. While prices rocket up, his thirteen cents can drop to zero. Or below. But when prices fall, nobody calls Mike to complain that his gas is too cheap. So prices drift down slowly. Like a feather. A federal study found retail prices went up more than four times faster than they came down. Economists literally call this "rockets and feathers." So here's a fun one. This fall, as prices came down, is exactly when station margins get wider. The cheaper your gas gets, the happier Mike gets. Just slower than you.
