The gas is the bait. Meet Mike. Mike is made up, but his numbers aren't. He owns one gas station off a highway in Ohio. His sign says four dollars and seventeen cents. That's about the national average as November started. Mike looks rich. Mike is not rich. Let's cut one gallon open and see who eats. The industry's own trade group did this math with a three-dollar gallon, so we'll use it. More than half of it is crude oil. About a dollar eighty-five. The refinery takes about forty cents. Taxes take about fifty more. Federal tax alone is eighteen point four cents, and Congress hasn't raised it since nineteen ninety-three. What's left for Mike is his markup: about thirty-five cents a gallon. Thirty-five cents! Mike's getting a boat. Not so fast. Out of those thirty-five cents, Mike pays a truck to haul the fuel in. Six cents. Then credit card fees. About eight cents a gallon. So on average, the card companies get more than eight cents a gallon. Without owning a pump, a tank, or a mop. Across the industry last year, card fees hit a record twenty-one billion dollars. About four and a half billion of that was charged on the taxes. Fees. On taxes. That stations just pass along to the government. Then the lights, the staff, the pumps wearing out, and the occasional guy who drives off without paying. What Mike actually keeps: about thirteen cents a gallon. On a good day, a dime and change.
