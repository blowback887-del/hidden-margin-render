That missing dollar sign has a name in the business. Menu engineering. There are consultants who do this for a living, and one well-known menu engineer, Gregg Rapp, tells clients to drop the dollar signs, the decimal points and the cents.

The researchers think the reason is simple. A dollar sign reminds you that you're paying. Take it away, and fourteen is just a number. A nice, friendly number.

Now look at the top of the page. A tomahawk steak. Fifty-eight. Almost nobody orders the fifty-eight dollar steak. That's the point.

Writer William Poundstone calls it an anchor. A ridiculously expensive item they don't expect anyone to buy. Its job is to sit there and make everything below it look like a bargain. Next to fifty-eight, fourteen feels like a steal.

Notice the prices aren't lined up in a neat column, either. Poundstone says that's on purpose. Give people a column of prices, and the cheap ones run down it and pick from the three cheapest things.

Then the server comes over. Great choice. Want to add bacon? Three dollars. Truffle fries instead of regular? Two more.

That question is not small talk. Toast, a company whose system runs a huge number of restaurants, publishes training advice for restaurants. It says the register itself can remind staff to ask those upselling questions. It even suggests a leaderboard for the server who sells the most add-ons. [PAUSE]

And here's the principle behind this whole video. A menu price isn't the price. It's the opening bid. Like an airline's base fare. The real number gets built after you've already said yes.
