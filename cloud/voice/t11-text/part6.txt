In 2008, the cheese answered that question. Prices for beef and cheese were climbing, and the owners of McDonald's restaurants were complaining that the double cheeseburger, at a dollar, just didn't work anymore.
So McDonald's came up with a solution. A new burger. Two patties, same bun, same everything. But one slice of cheese instead of two. They called it the McDouble. The double cheeseburger moved up to a dollar nineteen, and the McDouble took its place on the Dollar Menu. One analyst estimated that dropping the slice saved about six cents a sandwich. [pause]
Six cents. That's how tight the math had become.
A year later, Burger King did something worse. It told its restaurant owners to sell a double cheeseburger for a dollar. The owners had voted against it. Twice. Corporate did it anyway.
So the owners did something almost unheard of. The association representing about eighty percent of Burger King's U.S. franchise owners sued their own company. One of them, a franchisee from South Bend, Indiana named Dan Fitzpatrick, explained the math. Once you count all the costs, he said, each of those dollar burgers cost them between a dollar ten and a dollar fifteen to sell.
They were losing a dime on every single one. And the more popular the deal got, the more they lost.
By the spring of 2010, the burger had quietly moved up to a dollar twenty-nine. A judge threw the case out that fall. They settled the next year, and the owners didn't get any money.
And in 2013, McDonald's let go of the Dollar Menu itself. It turned into something called the Dollar Menu and More. Items at one dollar, two dollars, five dollars. Same idea, technically. But if you ever stood in front of that board, you know. It wasn't the same.
For years, the people who run the restaurants were eating the difference. And at some point, they stopped.
