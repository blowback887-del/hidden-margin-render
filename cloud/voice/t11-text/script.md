## PART 2
[VISUAL: a student counting coins; a worker eating in a truck cab; a mom with three kids at a drive-thru; an older man with a newspaper]
College kid with five bucks. Guy eating lunch in his truck. A parent feeding three kids from a drive-thru because nobody had the energy to cook. Or you just remember a Big Mac for under a dollar. Either way, this one's about you.
[VISUAL: angry headlines: "corporate greed", "price gouging"]
Now, there's an easy answer to why it all got so expensive. Greed. Some guy in a boardroom raised the price of your burger because he could. And look, that's not totally wrong. We'll get there. But it's not the part that surprised me.
[VISUAL: title card: HOW CHEAP WAS INVENTED]
Cheap food didn't just happen. It was invented, on purpose, for guys like the ones at that steel mill.
[VISUAL: Wichita, Kansas, 1921; a tiny white building that looks like a castle]
Wichita, Kansas. Nineteen twenty-one. A cook named Walter Anderson and a real estate man named Billy Ingram open a tiny restaurant that looks like a castle. A white castle.
[VISUAL: 1906 book cover of The Jungle; illustrations of filthy meatpacking plants]
They have a problem nobody today would think about. Americans are scared of hamburger. Back in 1906, a novel called The Jungle exposed what was going on inside the meatpacking plants. And ground beef got a reputation as the stuff you made out of whatever was left on the floor.
[VISUAL: White Castle exterior, white tile, steel, a cook grinding beef behind the counter in full view]
So White Castle doesn't just sell hamburgers. It sells trust. The building is white because white looks clean. The beef is ground right in front of you. [VISUAL: "5¢" painted on a window; "Buy 'em by the sack" sign]

Cheap food was never charity. It was a promise. You can trust this, and you can afford this. And that promise was about to get very, very big.

## PART 3
[VISUAL: San Bernardino, California, 1940s drive-in with carhops on roller skates]
San Bernardino, California. Nineteen forty-eight. Two brothers, Dick and Mac McDonald, run a drive-in restaurant that's already doing well. Carhops bring food out to your car. There's a long menu. Barbecue. All kinds of stuff.
[VISUAL: a "CLOSED" sign on the drive-in]
And then they do something that looks completely insane. They shut it down. For three months.
[VISUAL: menu shrinking from 25 items to 9]
When they reopen, the carhops are gone. The menu goes from about twenty-five items to nine. Paper wrappers instead of plates. No tipping. And the hamburger costs fifteen cents, about half what it used to.
[VISUAL: chalk lines on a tennis court, little figures moving like an assembly line]
They redesigned the kitchen like a factory line. As the story goes, they drew it out in chalk on a tennis court, and walked their crew through every step until it was perfect. Every worker had one job, and only one. They called it the Speedee Service System.
[VISUAL: profit chart dipping down; a fired carhop leaning on a car, laughing]
At first, it flopped. Profits dropped. The carhops they'd let go would come by just to make fun of them.
[VISUAL: chart climbing; lines of cars; "OVER 1 MILLION SOLD" sign]
And then it took off. Families showed up. Working people showed up. Kids with pocket change showed up. Dick McDonald later put the whole idea in one line. Our whole concept was based on speed, lower prices, and volume.
[VISUAL: three words light up: SPEED. LOW PRICE. VOLUME.]
That's the machine. Sell a lot of something cheap, very fast, to everybody. The lower the price, the bigger the crowd. The bigger the crowd, the cheaper you can make it.
[VISUAL: 1950s photo of Ray Kroc; then Harry Sonneborn; "Franchise Realty Corp."]
Then a milkshake machine salesman named Ray Kroc shows up, sees the line out the door, and turns it into an empire. But the guy you really need to know is his money man, Harry Sonneborn.
[VISUAL: quote card]
Sonneborn reportedly put it like this. We are not technically in the food business. We are in the real estate business. The only reason we sell fifteen-cent hamburgers is because they are the greatest producer of revenue from which our tenants can pay us rent. [PAUSE]
[VISUAL: a burger with a tiny house hidden inside it]
The cheap burger was never the product. It was the thing that brought people in the door, so the people running the restaurant could pay rent to McDonald's. That's going to matter a lot later.

## PART 4
[VISUAL: title card: THE PRICE WARS; 1989 Wendy's menu board with 99¢ items]
For the next few decades, the whole industry played by those rules. And whenever things got slow, everybody reached for the same lever. Price.
In 1989, Wendy's put a whole row of items on its menu for ninety-nine cents each. The value menu was born, and everybody copied it.
[VISUAL: 1997 newspaper ad: "BIG MAC 55¢"; Roanoke Times AP clipping]
And in 1997, McDonald's tried the boldest price cut in its history. They called it Campaign Fifty-Five, after 1955, the year the company was founded. The idea was simple. A Big Mac, which normally cost about a dollar ninety, for fifty-five cents.
[VISUAL: the fine print growing under the 55¢: "with purchase of fries and a drink"]
Except it wasn't simple. To get the fifty-five cent Big Mac, you had to buy fries and a drink too. And the deal changed from sandwich to sandwich. People walked up to the counter and didn't understand what they were supposed to order. Some of the restaurant owners said it was actually costing them money.
[VISUAL: Reuters June 1997: "McDonald's U.S. Marketing Plan in Question"; stock ticker dipping]
Burger King was already selling Whoppers for ninety-nine cents in some places. And about six weeks in, McDonald's announced it was killing the promotion for lunch and dinner. It was supposed to last all year. [PAUSE]
[VISUAL: two price tags: a simple "$1" and a tangled "55¢*" with a long asterisk]
Cheap only works when it's simple. A price you have to decode isn't a deal. It's homework. People don't want a puzzle. They want a number they can trust. Keep that in mind, because twenty-nine years later, McDonald's would learn this lesson all over again.

## PART 5
[VISUAL: title card: THE DOLLAR THAT SAVED McDONALD'S; then the 2000s, shiny Dollar Menu boards]
Now let's jump to the moment cheap fast food peaked. If you were a kid or a broke student in the 2000s, you remember this menu. The Dollar Menu.
[VISUAL: CBS News screenshot, January 2003: "McDeficit"]
What most people don't know is that the Dollar Menu wasn't born out of generosity. It was born out of panic.
At the end of 2002, McDonald's posted the first quarterly loss in its entire history. Three hundred and forty-three million dollars. It said it would close hundreds of restaurants. The stock fell to around fifteen dollars, the lowest in years. The most famous restaurant on earth was in real trouble.
[VISUAL: portrait of Jim Cantalupo; "came out of retirement" headline]
So the company called back a man who had already retired. Jim Cantalupo. He'd spent nearly thirty years at McDonald's. He came back as CEO on January first, 2003, and leaned on the oldest weapon the company owned. Cheap.
[VISUAL: Dollar Menu TV ads, early 2000s style; sales chart turning up]
The Dollar Menu had just gone national. Sandwiches, fries, drinks, a dollar each. And it worked. Customers came back. The turnaround became one of the big business stories of the decade.
[VISUAL: Orlando convention center at night; a single empty podium]
Sixteen months in, at a convention of McDonald's franchise owners in Orlando, Jim Cantalupo had a heart attack before dawn. He was sixty. He never saw what happened to the menu he bet on. [PAUSE]
[VISUAL: a family of four at a table full of dollar items; "UNDER $10" receipt]
The menu kept going. For about ten years, it was the floor of American life. A family of four could eat for under ten dollars. A teenager with a few quarters could eat like a king.
[VISUAL: a dollar bill, slowly getting heavier, a weight sliding onto it]
But here's the thing about a dollar. Every year, the beef behind it, the cheese, the bun, the paper, the people making it, all cost a little more. And the price on the sign stays exactly the same. Somebody has to absorb that difference. The only question is who.

## PART 6
[VISUAL: 2008 news: NRN, ABC News; a double cheeseburger, one slice of cheese lifting off and floating away]
In 2008, the cheese answered that question. Prices for beef and cheese were climbing, and the owners of McDonald's restaurants were complaining that the double cheeseburger, at a dollar, just didn't work anymore.
So McDonald's came up with a solution. A new burger. Two patties, same bun, same everything. But one slice of cheese instead of two. They called it the McDouble. The double cheeseburger moved up to a dollar nineteen, and the McDouble took its place on the Dollar Menu. One analyst estimated that dropping the slice saved about six cents a sandwich. [PAUSE]
[VISUAL: the two burgers side by side, the missing slice highlighted]
Six cents. That's how tight the math had become.
[VISUAL: 2009 Burger King headlines: "Franchisees sue Burger King over $1 burger"]
A year later, Burger King did something worse. It told its restaurant owners to sell a double cheeseburger for a dollar. The owners had voted against it. Twice. Corporate did it anyway.
[VISUAL: Nation's Restaurant News screenshot; quote card with Dan Fitzpatrick's name and city]
So the owners did something almost unheard of. The association representing about eighty percent of Burger King's U.S. franchise owners sued their own company. One of them, a franchisee from South Bend, Indiana named Dan Fitzpatrick, explained the math. Once you count all the costs, he said, each of those dollar burgers cost them between a dollar ten and a dollar fifteen to sell.
[VISUAL: a dollar going in, $1.10 going out; a little red "-10¢"]
They were losing a dime on every single one. And the more popular the deal got, the more they lost.
[VISUAL: court documents; then a menu board with "$1.29"]
By the spring of 2010, the burger had quietly moved up to a dollar twenty-nine. A judge threw the case out that fall. They settled the next year, and the owners didn't get any money.
[VISUAL: 2013, the Dollar Menu sign replaced by "Dollar Menu & More"]
And in 2013, McDonald's let go of the Dollar Menu itself. It turned into something called the Dollar Menu and More. Items at one dollar, two dollars, five dollars. Same idea, technically. But if you ever stood in front of that board, you know. It wasn't the same.
[VISUAL: a dollar bill, torn in half]
For years, the people who run the restaurants were eating the difference. And at some point, they stopped.

## PART 10
[VISUAL: title card: WHO LEFT; a drive-thru line, cars disappearing one by one]
And here's the part where the company itself confirms it.
[VISUAL: Restaurant Dive and LA Times screenshots; quote cards from earnings calls]
Early last year, McDonald's own CEO, Chris Kempczinski, told investors that low-income visits had fallen by nearly double digits. Later he said that across fast food, low-income visits were down and higher-income visits were up. His reasons? Rent. Groceries. Child care.
[VISUAL: a sign reading "Our most loyal customer" over an empty parking spot]
And here's the painful part. He also said low-income customers usually visit more often than anyone else. So the people who came the most look like the first ones who got pushed out.
[VISUAL: a phone with the McDonald's app; next to it, the menu board; two different prices]
And if you're the person counting every dollar, the system has a few extra traps waiting. Quiet ones.
[VISUAL: two McDonald's a few miles apart, two different price tags for the same meal]
Trap number one. The price on the board isn't the same everywhere. Franchise owners set their own prices. Two McDonald's a few miles apart can charge you different amounts for the same meal. That's how a rest stop in Connecticut ended up with a seventeen dollar Big Mac meal that went viral.
[VISUAL: app screen with "DEALS" tab glowing]
Trap number two. For years, the best prices didn't live on the board at all. They lived in the app. McDonald's now has about two hundred and ten million active users in its loyalty program around the world. If you don't use it, you're often paying the highest price in the building.
[VISUAL: delivery bag on a doorstep; receipt growing longer]
Trap number three. Delivery. A study by the finance company Self priced the same McDonald's order in a hundred American cities. Through the big delivery apps, it cost almost seventy percent more on average than buying it at the restaurant. Seventy percent. For somebody too tired to drive.
[VISUAL: AP headline Oct 2026: "McDonald's sued over AI tool that recommends prices"; "ALLEGATION" stamp]
And there's a new question hanging over all of it. In October, a customer in Illinois filed a lawsuit claiming McDonald's uses an AI pricing system to coordinate prices across its restaurants. That's an accusation. No court has ruled on it. McDonald's says, and I'm quoting, AI does not set menu prices at McDonald's restaurants. McDonald's franchisees do.
[VISUAL: the drive-thru line, thinner now]
And for a business built on volume, that's the real problem. The crowd was walking away.
