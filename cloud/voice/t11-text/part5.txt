Now let's jump to the moment cheap fast food peaked. If you were a kid or a broke student in the 2000s, you remember this menu. The Dollar Menu.
What most people don't know is that the Dollar Menu wasn't born out of generosity. It was born out of panic.
At the end of 2002, McDonald's posted the first quarterly loss in its entire history. Three hundred and forty-three million dollars. It said it would close hundreds of restaurants. The stock fell to around fifteen dollars, the lowest in years. The most famous restaurant on earth was in real trouble.
So the company called back a man who had already retired. Jim Cantalupo. He'd spent nearly thirty years at McDonald's. He came back as CEO on January first, 2003, and leaned on the oldest weapon the company owned. Cheap.
The Dollar Menu had just gone national. Sandwiches, fries, drinks, a dollar each. And it worked. Customers came back. The turnaround became one of the big business stories of the decade.
Sixteen months in, at a convention of McDonald's franchise owners in Orlando, Jim Cantalupo had a heart attack before dawn. He was sixty. He never saw what happened to the menu he bet on. [pause]
The menu kept going. For about ten years, it was the floor of American life. A family of four could eat for under ten dollars. A teenager with a few quarters could eat like a king.
But here's the thing about a dollar. Every year, the beef behind it, the cheese, the bun, the paper, the people making it, all cost a little more. And the price on the sign stays exactly the same. Somebody has to absorb that difference. The only question is who.
