San Bernardino, California. Nineteen forty-eight. Two brothers, Dick and Mac McDonald, run a drive-in restaurant that's already doing well. Carhops bring food out to your car. There's a long menu. Barbecue. All kinds of stuff.
And then they do something that looks completely insane. They shut it down. For three months.
When they reopen, the carhops are gone. The menu goes from about twenty-five items to nine. Paper wrappers instead of plates. No tipping. And the hamburger costs fifteen cents, about half what it used to.
They redesigned the kitchen like a factory line. As the story goes, they drew it out in chalk on a tennis court, and walked their crew through every step until it was perfect. Every worker had one job, and only one. They called it the Speedee Service System.
At first, it flopped. Profits dropped. The carhops they'd let go would come by just to make fun of them.
And then it took off. Families showed up. Working people showed up. Kids with pocket change showed up. Dick McDonald later put the whole idea in one line. Our whole concept was based on speed, lower prices, and volume.
That's the machine. Sell a lot of something cheap, very fast, to everybody. The lower the price, the bigger the crowd. The bigger the crowd, the cheaper you can make it.
Then a milkshake machine salesman named Ray Kroc shows up, sees the line out the door, and turns it into an empire. But the guy you really need to know is his money man, Harry Sonneborn.
Sonneborn reportedly put it like this. We are not technically in the food business. We are in the real estate business. The only reason we sell fifteen-cent hamburgers is because they are the greatest producer of revenue from which our tenants can pay us rent. [pause]
The cheap burger was never the product. It was the thing that brought people in the door, so the people running the restaurant could pay rent to McDonald's. That's going to matter a lot later.
