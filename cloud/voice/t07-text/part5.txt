In June, three California drivers filed a federal lawsuit against some of the biggest names at the pump. BP. Circle K. Marathon. 7-Eleven. Walmart. Albertsons. Together, more than seventeen hundred stations.

The lawsuit is about software. A pricing system from a company called Kalibrate. According to the complaint, it connects straight to the stations' pumps and signs, and uses data from competing stations to set prices.

The drivers call it the central nervous system for a conspiracy. And the complaint says the software discourages stations from pricing below their rivals. Undercut the guy across the street, and it warns you about a downward spiral. [PAUSE]

Picture two stations on the same corner, both feeding their numbers into the same system. The price war you'd expect never comes.

According to the lawsuit, where stations use it, gas costs about six cents more on average. And up to thirty cents in some markets. And every extra cent costs California drivers about a hundred thirty-four million dollars a year.

Remember the ten to fifteen cents a station makes on a gallon? This lawsuit says the software pushed prices up as much as thirty. [PAUSE]

California had just passed a law that says sharing a pricing algorithm with competitors can count as price fixing. It took effect in January. These are allegations. Walmart said it was reviewing the complaint, BP didn't comment, and a court will decide.
