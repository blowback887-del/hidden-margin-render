Start with the sign out on the road. Three ninety-nine. Look closer. In small letters, under it, one word. Cash.

The card price is often five to twenty cents more a gallon. Most people pay with a card. In Maryland, a state senator called that sign bait and switch. Since October 2024, Maryland stations have to show the card price on the sign, in numbers the same size.

Other states don't bother. In Florida, a TV station found a Chevron advertising three fifteen on the road and charging a full dollar more per gallon on a card. Legal. [PAUSE]

A week after the reporter started asking questions, the gap came down. To ninety cents. Months later, it was back to a dollar.

Why charge extra for a card at all? Because every swipe costs the station money. The trade group says bank fees eat six to ten cents of every gallon. Last year, convenience stores paid a record twenty-one point three billion dollars in card fees. For many of them, the trade group says, that's more than their pre-tax profit.

Then there's that little nine at the end. Nine tenths of a cent. It started in the 1930s, when gas cost pennies and a full cent was a big jump. It never went away. The trade group itself says that fraction can be as much as a tenth of what a station makes on fuel.

So if the gas barely pays, what does? Here's the whole business in one line. The gallon is the bait. The store is the business.

Fuel brings in sixty-five percent of a convenience store's sales, but less than forty percent of its gross profit. The trade group has spent years telling owners to pay the whole site's bills with what they make inside. And inside, the biggest profit maker is food and fountain drinks. Almost thirty-nine percent of in-store gross profit.

The store is built for that. Industry design advice calls the drink cooler a traffic generator, and says to place it so it pulls you through the store. Go in for a beer, and you grab something else on the way back to the register. You came for gas. You leave with a hot dog.

But you don't even have to go inside to overpay. There's a button on the pump for that.
