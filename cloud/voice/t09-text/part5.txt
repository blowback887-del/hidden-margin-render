Years before the outlet lawsuits, a man named Antonio Hinojos took Kohl's to court. He'd bought a Samsonite suitcase marked fifty percent off its original price of two ninety-nine ninety-nine. His claim was that Kohl's routinely sold that merchandise at the sale price.

A lower court threw the case out. It said he got what he wanted, at the price advertised, so where's the harm? [PAUSE]

In twenty thirteen, the federal appeals court disagreed. To shoppers, it said, the original price matters. Or in the court's own short words: price advertisements matter.

In December twenty sixteen, the city of Los Angeles sued four of the biggest names in American retail. J.C. Penney. Kohl's. Macy's. And Sears.

One example from the lawsuit against Sears. A washing machine advertised with an original price of eleven seventy-nine ninety-nine. The city said that in roughly its first six months online, Sears never offered it for more than nine ninety-nine ninety-nine.

So, according to the city, the original price was higher than any price it actually had. [PAUSE]

Or take Macy's. The city said a cross pendant necklace went online with an original price of a hundred and twenty dollars, and a sale price of thirty. Seventy-five percent off, from the day it went on sale. And in the five months after, it never sold for more than thirty. [PAUSE]

Off-price stores have their own version. T.J. Maxx puts a comparison price on its tags. When shoppers sued in twenty fifteen, its own website described that number as its buying staff's estimate.

ABC News checked one Michael Kors purse in a T.J. Maxx. The tag said compare at three twenty-eight. The maker's suggested price was two twenty-eight.

T.J. Maxx settled, and a judge approved it in twenty eighteen. It agreed to explain its numbers better and put up more signs. The comparison prices are still on the tags.
