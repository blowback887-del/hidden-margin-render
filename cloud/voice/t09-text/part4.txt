Now the big number. Who actually writes it?

In twenty fourteen, a shopper named Tressa Gattinella sued Michael Kors over a pair of pants from its outlet. The tag said MSRP, a hundred and twenty dollars. Our price, seventy-nine ninety-nine.

MSRP means manufacturer's suggested retail price. Her lawsuit said those pants were made only for the outlet. So the price was suggested for a store where they were never sold.

Michael Kors denied doing anything wrong. But in twenty fifteen it agreed to pay four point eight eight million dollars to settle. And it agreed to change the tags.

Here's how. It would stop using the letters MSRP. And replace them with one word. Value. Or drop the big number on outlet-only items. [PAUSE]

A new word for the same number.

Eleven years later, look at the tag on our bag again. Comparable value.

Comparable to what? In Oregon, the rules say a compared price should come from a real competitor in the same area, selling similar goods. The lawsuit says Kate Spade sets that number the same way across the whole country. And it points to a competitor in the same outlet center charging ninety-five twenty for a similar tote. [PAUSE]

Same story with a wallet from that store. Comparable value, two thirty-nine. After an extra twenty-five percent off, fifty-nine twenty-five. And the lawsuit says a similar leather wallet at Michael Kors in the same center was forty-seven twenty.

It gets stranger. Checkbook found a Banana Republic Factory shirtdress with a compare at price of one forty. The real cotton dress at the regular store was one ninety-eight. So the number on the tag matched neither dress.

And the Tommy Hilfiger lawsuit goes further. The lawyers say they checked California outlet stores on and off from twenty twenty-two to twenty twenty-five. And not once did they see a product offered at its full original price. Hundreds, maybe thousands, of products, marked on sale the whole time. [PAUSE]

A sale that never ends isn't a sale. It's just the price.
